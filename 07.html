What factors are most strongly associated with diabetes in this dataset? Show me the key findings with charts and figures?

1. Executive Summary

The clinical meaning of the recorded diagnosis values was not independently verified. Within the available 1,879 records, however, the strongest signals linked to the recorded diagnosis outcome are clearly fasting blood sugar and HbA1c, followed by frequent urination, hypertension, unexplained weight loss, and excessive thirst.

Decision-critical message. Use elevated glycemic measures as the primary trigger for confirmatory review and outreach. Use recognizable symptoms and hypertension to help prioritize attention, rather than as substitutes for clinical review.
  • Glycemic measures lead the evidence. Records with diagnosis value 1 had substantially higher typical fasting blood sugar and HbA1c readings than records with value 0.
  • Frequent urination is the strongest practical symptom signal. Among 371 records where it was reported, 55.0% carried diagnosis value 1; unexplained weight loss and excessive thirst were also linked to recorded diagnosis rates near one-half.
  • Hypertension merits an integrated workflow. It was the leading non-laboratory characteristic in the combined prioritization, supporting coordinated metabolic screening during hypertension-related care.
  • The evidence supports prioritization, not diagnosis or prevention claims. The timing of measurements, symptoms, treatment, and diagnosis is unknown, and the records do not show that any observed characteristic causes diabetes.

2. Business Context

For screening, care-management, and population-health leaders, the central question is which recorded signals can focus scarce follow-up capacity on people most likely to warrant confirmatory review. Of the 1,879 records assessed, 752 (40.0%) carried recorded diagnosis value 1 and 1,127 (60.0%) carried value 0.

The decision boundary is deliberately narrow: this report identifies associations with the recorded outcome, not clinical diagnostic rules, treatment effects, prevention effects, or an individual risk score. Because the diagnosis values are stored as 0 and 1 without independently confirmed clinical semantics, findings should be operationally validated before use in patient-facing or clinical decision support.

3. Analysis Approach

The assessment reviewed the available record set across 44 patient characteristics and compared which signals remained most useful when considered alongside the other recorded characteristics. It also examined the two leading clinical measures, symptom-positive groups, and recorded diagnosis patterns across fasting blood sugar bands.

Evidence coverage. The completed work supports a combined prioritization ranking, clinical-measure comparisons, and symptom-group rates. It does not provide the requested evidence on whether having both glycemic measures elevated adds value beyond one elevated measure, or whether symptoms add value among people with similar glycemic readings. Broad group-rate comparisons for lifestyle, demographic, treatment, and environmental factors were also not completed.

Interpretation limits. The record grain and patient uniqueness were not confirmed. Measures reflect status at collection rather than a sequence over time. Symptoms and several other measures may be self-reported; medication use can reflect existing illness and care decisions; and demographic codes lack business-readable labels. Results are based on 1,879 records and may not generalize beyond this population.

4. Key Findings

Glycemic measures are the clear first-line signals

Evidence. Fasting blood sugar was the strongest recorded signal in the combined prioritization, with HbA1c close behind. Both measures were available for all 1,879 records. Typical fasting blood sugar was 157.4 for the diagnosis-value-1 group versus 113.4 for the value-0 group. Typical HbA1c was 7.9 versus 6.0.

Interpretation. The consistent separation across both measures suggests they should anchor screening, outreach, and care-management prioritization. The groups still overlap, so an elevated result should support clinical review rather than function as a standalone determination.

Symptoms provide recognizable supplemental triage signals

Evidence. Frequent urination ranked third overall in the combined prioritization. Among records where each symptom was recorded as present, diagnosis-value-1 rates ranged from 40.9% to 55.0%; none of these groups was flagged as too small for interpretation.

These figures do not show the added value of symptoms after accounting for comparable glycemic readings.

Hypertension is a leading non-laboratory signal

Evidence. Hypertension was the fourth-strongest higher recorded-diagnosis signal in the combined prioritization, behind fasting blood sugar, HbA1c, and frequent urination, and ahead of unexplained weight loss.

Interpretation. Hypertension-related encounters appear to be a high-value point at which to consider metabolic screening or follow-up. This relationship should not be read as evidence that hypertension causes diabetes; it may reflect shared patient characteristics, recognition of illness, or care patterns.

Recorded diagnosis rises sharply across most higher fasting blood sugar bands

Evidence. The share of records carrying diagnosis value 1 rose from about 9% in the lowest displayed fasting blood sugar band to about 29% in the next band, 59% in the following band, and 64% in the highest displayed band.

Interpretation. Elevated fasting blood sugar bands are a practical starting point for record review and outreach prioritization. The pattern also identifies a population without a recorded diagnosis in elevated bands that merits validation for follow-up, documentation, repeat measurement, and treatment context before any conclusion about a care gap is drawn.

Some apparent lower associations are not action-ready

The ranking also includes lower recorded-diagnosis associations for certain coded ethnicity values, a prior-history indicator, and statin use. These results should not drive targeting or be interpreted as protective effects. Ethnicity codes cannot be interpreted without a codebook, and medication use can reflect treatment and care-management context rather than an underlying risk factor.

5. Implications

The evidence supports a layered operational approach: lead with fasting blood sugar and HbA1c for confirmatory review; use frequent urination, excessive thirst, unexplained weight loss, and hypertension to focus outreach and clinician attention; and reserve coded demographic, medication, lifestyle, and environmental signals for further validation. This can improve focus of screening and care-management activity, but it should not be implemented as an automated diagnosis rule because the available records do not establish clinical label definitions, timing, incremental signal value, or downstream outcomes.

6. Recommendations

  • Prioritize glycemic-review workflows. Direct confirmatory review and outreach capacity first toward elevated fasting blood sugar and HbA1c results, subject to clinical governance and approved thresholds.
  • Add symptom and hypertension prompts. Use frequent urination, excessive thirst, unexplained weight loss, and hypertension as supplemental cues in screening and care-management workflows.
  • Validate elevated-measure records without a recorded diagnosis. Review follow-up status, repeat measures, care context, and documentation for people in elevated fasting blood sugar bands who do not carry diagnosis value 1.
  • Close the two decision-critical evidence gaps. Compare records with both glycemic measures elevated against those with only one elevated measure, and assess whether symptoms identify higher recorded-diagnosis rates among people with similar glycemic readings.
  • Establish governance before broader targeting. Confirm the clinical meaning of diagnosis values, patient-record uniqueness, timing of key events, and demographic code definitions; then complete group-rate reviews for lifestyle, treatment, demographic, and environmental characteristics.
